When Standing Becomes the Issue: What the Evidence Says About Separating People from Problems on the Floor

When Standing Becomes the Issue: What the Evidence Says About Separating People from Problems on the Floor

Frontline negotiations fail for a reason that rarely shows up on the work order: the people become the problem. A late truck turns into a fight about who was disrespected. A coverage ask turns into a referendum on competence. Once standing and substance fuse, neither gets resolved.

Attribution and escalation

People explain their own behavior by circumstance and others' by character — a pattern documented across attribution research (Jones & Nisbett, 1971). On a dock, that means "I held the gate because receiving was flooded" becomes "they're being difficult" when the other side does the same thing. The substantive issue (a flooded dock) gets replaced by a character judgment that hardens positions.

Face and the side agenda

Goffman's analysis of face-work shows that protecting public image can become a goal of its own, separate from the original task (Goffman, 1967). A supervisor who feels corrected in front of the crew is now negotiating two agendas: the coverage number and the need not to look weak. Collapsing them into one fight usually means both sides dig in.

Constructive conflict needs a split

Mary Parker Follett argued that conflict becomes useful only when parties treat disagreement as a shared problem rather than a personal contest (Follett, 1940). Modern negotiation analysis echoes this: parties who deliberately separate interpersonal friction from the issue under discussion reach more durable agreements (Walton & McKersie, 1965; Pruitt, 1981).

What frontline leaders can check

Three practical markers: (1) the exchange is sharper than the stakes justify; (2) you are defending standing more than the problem; (3) old history is being pulled into a narrow dispute. When any marker appears, name the personal stake out loud and separately, then restate the substantive problem in one plain sentence and address only that.

Selected references

  • Follett, M. P. (1940). Constructive conflict. In H. C. Metcalf & L. Urwick (Eds.), Dynamic Administration. Harper & Brothers.
  • Goffman, E. (1967). Interaction Ritual. Doubleday.
  • Jones, E. E., & Nisbett, R. E. (1971). The actor and the observer. In E. E. Jones et al. (Eds.), Attribution. General Learning Press.
  • Pruitt, D. G. (1981). Negotiation Behavior. Academic Press.
  • Walton, R. E., & McKersie, R. B. (1965). A Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations. McGraw-Hill.

The Radio Call That Was About Me, Not the Delay

The Radio Call That Was About Me, Not the Delay

I almost keyed the radio to prove a point.

A peer on second shift held an operator I needed for a changeover. The delay was real. So was the tone in my voice when I asked again. By the third exchange I was no longer negotiating for a certified person — I was negotiating for not looking like I'd been ignored in front of my crew.

A lead who overheard asked me one question: "Are you fighting the delay or fighting the tone?" That split cooled me enough to call back and say, "The tone landed hard. Separate from that, I still need one certified operator by 14:00 for the changeover — what options do you have?" We found a swap. The delay shrank. My standing recovered because I stopped making it the main issue.

I still get hooked. The habit that saves me is naming the personal stake out loud before I restate the ask.

Case 1 — Nora and the Handoff That Got Personal

Nora leads first shift packaging. At handoff, second-shift lead Marco says her crew left the line "a mess again." Nora hears a competence attack. She answers with a list of everything second shift left unfinished last week. Ten minutes later they are arguing history, not the changeover checklist sitting between them.

The substantive issue is a missed cleaning step that will delay startup. The personal stake is Nora feeling publicly blamed.

Discussion prompts

Framework — People/Problem Split Card

Framework — People/Problem Split Card

Use this when a shift, dock, or vendor exchange gets sharper than the stakes.

Step 1 — Marker check (any yes = pause)

  • Exchange sharper than stakes?
  • Defending standing more than the problem?
  • Old history pulled into a narrow issue?

Step 2 — Split aloud

  1. Personal stake (one sentence): "What's landing for me is ___."
  2. Substantive problem (one sentence): "Separate from that, the issue is ___."

Step 3 — Address each track

  • Standing: acknowledge or repair without trading the substantive ask.
  • Substance: interests, options, criteria — not volume.

Step 4 — Close

Confirm both tracks got a next step, or name which one is deferred and why.

Key Notes — Naming the Personal Stake Before You Fight the Problem

Key Notes — Naming the Personal Stake Before You Fight the Problem

Unit goal: Notice when ego, tone, or history has fused with a floor dispute, separate that personal stake from the substantive problem, and address each on its own terms.

Core ideas

  • Floor fights escalate when standing and substance fuse (Follett, 1940; Goffman, 1967).
  • You will explain your behavior by circumstance and theirs by character unless you check it (Jones & Nisbett, 1971).
  • Naming the personal stake out loud is not weakness — it clears the table for the real problem.
  • Restate the substantive issue in one plain sentence after the split.

Frontline checklist

  1. Notice when the exchange is sharper than the stakes.
  2. Ask: am I defending the problem or my standing?
  3. Write one circumstance-based explanation for the other side you have not checked.
  4. Name the personal stake separately, then restate the substantive ask.
  5. Resolve standing and substance on separate tracks if both still matter.

Reflection
