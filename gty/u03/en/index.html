Behind the Number: What the Evidence Says About Interests Hiding in Floor Positions

Behind the Number: What the Evidence Says About Interests Hiding in Floor Positions

A stated position is a demand. An interest is the need the demand is trying to protect. Frontline leaders who only counter numbers stay stuck; leaders who ask what the number is for open room to move.

Positions vs interests

Fisher and Ury's popular framing is widely known, but the underlying research tradition is older: integrative bargainers who focus on underlying needs outperform pure distributive tactics (Walton & McKersie, 1965; Pruitt, 1981). On a floor, "I need four people" may protect training continuity, a promised break pattern, or fear of missing a ship window — none of which appear in the number.

Questions that surface interests

Targeted questions — why this matters, what problem it solves, what happens if unmet — reliably increase information exchange and option generation (Pruitt, 1981; Thompson, 1991). Supervisors who ask before countering spend less time in deadlocked restatement.

Multiple options beat a single counter

Once interests are named, generating two or more packages that could satisfy both sides improves settlement quality (Pruitt, 1981). A single counter-position restarts the distributive fight.

Floor application

Before restating your ask: write the other side's position, guess the interest, ask one probe, then sketch two options. Re-check your walk-away if the talk stalls (Sebenius, 1992).

Selected references

  • Pruitt, D. G. (1981). Negotiation Behavior. Academic Press.
  • Sebenius, J. K. (1992). Negotiation analysis: A characterization and review. Management Science, 38(1), 18–38.
  • Thompson, L. (1991). Information exchange in negotiation. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 27(2), 161–179.
  • Walton, R. E., & McKersie, R. B. (1965). A Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations. McGraw-Hill.

The Overtime Number That Wasn't About Hours

The Overtime Number That Wasn't About Hours

A peer insisted on "no OT past 18:00 — period." I counter-offered 19:00. We stalled.

When I finally asked what 18:00 protected, the answer was childcare pickup, not cost. We built a split crew: two stayed to 19:30 with Saturday flex; the rest left on time. Same ship window. Different interest.

I wasted twenty minutes treating a schedule as a number fight. The probe should have been first.

Case 1 — Priya and the Fixed Headcount Demand

Case 1 — Priya and the Fixed Headcount Demand

Priya needs two temps for a surge. The agency contact holds at "minimum four or nothing." Priya counters with two. Deadlock.

Interest behind four: the agency's dispatch already scheduled a van for four and will eat cost otherwise. Options: Priya takes four for two days and releases early; or shares two with a sister line.

Discussion prompts

  1. What probe surfaces the van constraint?
  2. Which option protects both interests?

Key Notes — Asking Why Before You Counter

Key Notes — Asking Why Before You Counter

Unit goal: Dig past opening positions to uncover interests and generate more than one workable option.

Core ideas

  • Positions are demands; interests are needs (Pruitt, 1981).
  • Ask why / what it solves before you counter.
  • Two options beat one counter-position.
  • Log which interest actually unlocked the deal.

Frontline checklist

  1. Write both positions in one line each.
  2. Guess the interest behind each.
  3. Ask one probe before restating your number.
  4. Sketch two mutual-gain options.
  5. Only then propose.
